Binary Numbers Explained

Binary, explained simply

Computers count with just two digits.

People use ten digits, 0 through 9. Computers use only two: 0 and 1. That system is called binary, and each single digit is called a bit.

Think of light switches

Picture a row of light switches. Off means 0 and on means 1. Flip a few of them and you have a pattern. Every pattern stands for a number.

Play with the switches

Tap a switch to turn it on or off. Each switch is worth double the one to its right, starting with 1 on the far right.

Counting in binary

Counting works the same way as with ten digits, except each place fills up at 2 instead of 10. When a place is full, it resets to 0 and adds one to the place on its left.

Notice that 2 is written as 10. In binary, that means one 2 and no 1s. It does not mean ten.

Turning binary into a number

Take the binary number 1011 as an example.

8421 1011
  1. Write down the value of each place: 8, 4, 2, 1.
  2. Find the places that have a 1 under them. Here that's 8, 2, and 1.
  3. Add those values: 8 + 2 + 1 = 11.

Why 8 bits matter

A group of 8 bits is called a byte. It's a common unit of storage and the smallest chunk many computers work with at once.

256different patterns with 8 bits
0 to 255the range of numbers one byte can hold
11111111the largest 8-bit number, which is 255

Quick rule: every extra bit doubles how many numbers you can make.
